Patient No-Show Prediction Model: From Guessing to Strategic Double-Booking

A six-provider clinic loses nearly one booked slot in five to no-shows. Padding every session wastes patients' time; double-booking from memory is inconsistent. This project scores every appointment for no-show risk, then tests, on months the model never saw, which double-booking policy actually pays.

01 · The decisionWhich appointments do we double-book, and how many?

An empty slot is clinician time that cannot be recovered. A double-booked slot where both patients attend costs someone a wait. The value of any extra booking depends on one number: how likely the original patient is to miss.

02 · Where the risk livesNo-show risk is concentrated, not spread evenly

The clinic average hides a wide spread. Appointments booked more than 60 days ahead are missed more than twice as often as those booked within three days, and a patient with three or more earlier misses is missed more than twice as often as one with none.

03 · The modelA simple model gets most of the available signal, and its probabilities can be trusted

Two models were trained on the same features and compared on the test months. Logistic regression and gradient boosting tied, so the simpler, explainable one was kept. The last bar is the ceiling: the score a model would get if it knew each patient's true risk.

The model reaches an AUC of __AUC__, against __AUC_RULE__ for the front-desk rule of ranking patients by their own miss history, and a ceiling of __ORACLE__. Calibration matters more than ranking here. The break-even rule only works if a predicted 40% really means about 40%, and in the riskiest decile the model predicted __CAL1_P__ and the clinic observed __CAL1_O__.

04 · What drives the scoreA patient's own history does most of the work

Importance is measured by shuffling one input at a time on the test months and recording how much ranking quality falls.

05 · The policiesTargeting turns double-booking from a loss into a gain

Each policy may add at most two extra patients per provider-day. All are replayed on the same __PD_TEST__ held-out provider-days using the real attendance outcomes. Figures are per 100 provider-days.

Padding two random slots recovers __REC_FLAT__ slots but causes __COL_FLAT__ collisions, so only __HIT_FLAT__ of extra bookings land in a real gap. The model policy recovers __REC_MODEL__ slots with __COL_MODEL__ collisions (__HIT_MODEL__ hit rate) and nets $__MODEL_NET__. The front-desk rule is the surprise: it nets $__GUT_NET__, so a patient's miss history already carries most of the value. The model adds $__DIFF__ more per 100 provider-days (about __DIFF_PCT__), roughly $__ANN_DIFF__ a year for six providers. Most of the gain comes from replacing blanket padding with any targeted rule; the model adds precision on top.

06 · How many, and above what riskThe threshold is set by economics; the cap is a service guardrail

The break-even rule was set from the cost assumptions before any tuning, and the curves agree with it: net value peaked at a risk of __VAL_OPT__ on the validation months and __TEST_OPT__ on the test months, against a calculated __PSTAR__. The cap behaves differently. Each extra booking above the threshold still pays, but returns shrink (about $__MARG_12__ per added booking going from a cap of 1 to 2, and $__MARG_34__ from 3 to 4) while collisions keep rising. The cap is therefore a limit on patient waiting, not a profit lever. On the test months, __SHARE_PD__ of provider-days had at least one appointment above the line, with __FLAGS_PD__ on average.

07 · Stress testDoes the answer survive different economics?

The costs are assumptions, so each was moved in turn. For the model policy the threshold is recalculated for each scenario from its own break-even.

The model policy is positive in __SENS_MODEL__ of __SENS_N__ scenarios. The front-desk rule is positive in __SENS_GUT__ of __SENS_N__ and flat padding in only __SENS_FLAT__. The scenario that hurts most is a collision cost of $90, where the rule of thumb turns negative and the model's higher threshold keeps it positive. The more a collision costs, the more precision is worth.

08 · Who gets flaggedFlags are not evenly spread, and the policy has to allow for that

An accurate model can still place a burden unevenly. This check compares who is flagged with who is booked, on the test months.

Medicaid patients are __MCAID_A__ of appointments but __MCAID_F__ of flags, and patients aged 18 to 30 are __YOUNG_A__ of appointments but __YOUNG_F__ of flags. Medicare patients, who attend reliably, are only __MEDICARE_F__ of flags. The flags are about equally precise across groups: between __PREC_LO__ and __PREC_HI__ of flagged appointments were missed in every group. That means the model is not wrongly accusing one group, but the load of a possible wait still falls more often on some groups. A flag should add a booking, never remove one.

09 · What a clinic should doRecommendations

  1. Stop padding at random. It was the only policy that lost money in most scenarios, because only __HIT_FLAT__ of its extra bookings landed in a real gap.
  2. Start with the front-desk rule, then add the model. A patient's miss history captured most of the value. Use the model where the clinic can show the extra precision is worth the effort.
  3. Set the threshold from the clinic's own costs. Put a number on an idle slot and a collision, and double-book only above C / (I + C). Re-check it when either cost changes.
  4. Cap extra bookings at two per provider per day. Returns shrink and collisions climb past that, so treat the cap as a limit on waiting.
  5. Protect the flagged patient. Keep their appointment time, and make the short-notice patient the one who waits. Review flag rates by insurance and age group each month.
  6. Validate on your own data before use. Real no-show patterns, waitlist behaviour and costs will differ, and the first live month should be treated as a trial.
