Healthcare Resource Optimization: Beds, Staffing & Equipment

A 280-bed hospital heads into winter with a nurse roster sized to last year's average, a bed map drawn years ago and an equipment fleet nobody has re-counted. This project forecasts demand, then uses optimization and scenario modelling to show where capacity is being wasted and where it is about to run out.

Synthetic data. No real patient or hospital data is used. A patient-flow simulator generated 3.75 years of daily operations (Jan 2023 – Sep 2026) for three units, with plausible seasonality, weekday patterns, demand shocks and length-of-stay distributions. All costs are illustrative assumptions (see Method). Findings show how the method works, not what any specific hospital should do.

01 · The decisionWhat capacity do we need, and where is it being wasted?

Three resources, one shared driver. Bed demand sets nursing demand and equipment demand, so the project forecasts demand once and pushes the same simulated futures through all three decision models.

02 · ForecastingWinter runs hot: __MEANOCC__ average occupancy, __DAYS95__ days at ≥95%

Forecast for __WINDOW__. The band is the 5th–95th percentile of 500 simulated paths, so it includes random arrivals and length-of-stay variation, not just model error.

03 · StaffingA flat roster is wrong every day of the week

The current roster staffs the same number of nurses each day, sized to last year's average need. With winter demand and a 6% sick-call rate, that leaves a gap every day, filled with agency shifts at 1.75× the cost of a core shift. The optimiser chooses how many nurses to hire onto each days-off pattern so coverage tracks the weekday demand shape.

Result (on fresh holdout scenarios): weekly nursing cost falls by roughly __ROSTER_SAVE__ over the 26 weeks, agency shifts drop from about __AG_CUR__ to __AG_OPT__ per week, and the roster grows from 175 to 197 core nurses. The saving comes from substituting cheaper core hours for premium agency hours, so it assumes those nurses can be hired and retained.

04 · BedsSame 280 beds, redistributed: fewer lost ICU admissions

A linear program re-splits total beds across units to minimise expected diversion cost, with a conversion limit on how far any unit can move. ICU turns away the costliest patients and runs at the cap, so beds move toward it and Step-Down, and away from Med-Surg.

Honest read: total diversions rise slightly (a few more Med-Surg patients turned away) while expensive ICU and Step-Down diversions fall, so expected cost drops by about __BED_SAVE__ over the window. Allocation __ALLOC_CUR__ → __ALLOC_OPT__ (ICU / Step-Down / Med-Surg). ICU lands on its upper bound, which means the real constraint is physical conversion capacity, a finding in itself. This model does not fix the underlying shortfall: winter demand needs more total capacity or shorter stays (see scenarios).

05 · EquipmentVentilators are over-bought; infusion pumps are about to run short

Fleet size is a trade-off between ownership cost and stock-out cost. Simulated daily demand gives the probability of running out at each fleet size, and the cheapest size follows from that curve.

Pooling pays: if each unit kept its own pump fleet sized for 1% stock-outs, the hospital would need 420 pumps. A shared pool needs __PUMP_NEED__, about __PUMP_POOLSAVE__ fewer, because units rarely peak on the same day. For ventilators, the cost-optimal fleet of __VENT_OPT__ would save about __VENT_SAVE__ a year in ownership cost. Because a +10% surge pushes 99th-percentile demand higher, treat __VENT_OPT__ as a floor and keep a reserve rather than selling down to it.

06 · Scenario modellingWhat happens when the winter is worse, or we fix discharges?

Each scenario re-runs 300 simulated winters. Costs combine diversion losses, nursing (core roster plus agency gaps) and flex-bed overhead. The optimised plan is held fixed in the surge scenarios, so they show how robust it is, not what a re-optimised plan could do.

07 · RecommendationsWhat I would put in front of the executive team

  1. Re-base the nurse roster on the winter forecast, not last year's mean. Hire to a weekday-shaped pattern (about 197 core RNs vs 175). Expected saving about __ROSTER_SAVE__ for the winter window; confirm hiring lead time first.
  2. Make length of stay the first capacity programme. Half a day off average stay does more than any bed addition tested, and costs no beds. Start with discharge planning on Med-Surg, the largest unit.
  3. Shift 9 beds from Med-Surg toward ICU and Step-Down. Check the physical conversion limit first, because ICU hit its cap in the model.
  4. Buy about 45 infusion pumps and manage them as one pool. The current pool is short on roughly one day in three; pooling avoids buying about 35 more.
  5. Right-size ventilators, but keep a reserve. Redeploy surplus to a reserve or sister site instead of disposing of it.
  6. Treat flex beds as an insurance decision. The numbers say break-even; trigger them on a forecast occupancy threshold rather than opening them for the whole winter.
