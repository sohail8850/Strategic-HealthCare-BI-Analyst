Length-of-Stay (LOS) Prediction / Cost-of-Care Modeling

The problem

Hospitals lose money and beds in predictable ways: a small share of patients account for a disproportionate share of cost, and long-stay patients are usually identifiable early — if anyone is looking. Two operational questions drive this project: which admissions are likely to become long stays, so discharge planning and bed management can start on day one instead of day seven; and which admissions are likely to be expensive, so care management and utilization review can prioritize the right patients. This project builds an end-to-end pipeline — SQL data modeling → exploratory analysis → machine learning → BI dashboard — to answer both questions using only information available at the moment of admission, since that's the only point at which a prediction is operationally useful.

The approach

A star schema (dim_patients, dim_payers, fact_encounters) feeds a wide admission-time-only feature table — deliberately excluding anything only known mid-stay, such as procedures performed or diagnoses made after admission, since a model that "predicts" LOS using information from partway through the stay isn't deployable at the decision point that matters. Three targets were modeled — LOS regression, extended-stay classification (LOS > 7 days), and cost regression — each comparing a baseline (linear/logistic regression) against XGBoost, with SHAP used for explainability. Both LOS and cost are heavily right-skewed and were modeled on a log1p() transform accordingly. The data is synthetic, generated with Synthea (500 simulated Massachusetts patients) — there is no PHI or HIPAA scope here, but the pipeline is built as if it eventually needs to meet that bar.

The result

XGBoost improved LOS prediction MAE by 14.4% over the linear baseline (0.330 vs. 0.385 days). For extended-stay screening — a genuinely hard problem with ~4,400 admissions and only 16 positive cases in the test set — a recall-prioritized threshold caught 13 of 16 true extended-stay cases (81% recall) while flagging 9.2% of encounters for review (15% precision): a usable screening tool, not a diagnostic one. The cost model was intentionally the weakest of the three (MAE 77.1% of mean cost) because an admission-time-only model can't see the procedures and imaging that actually drive cost — reported honestly rather than masked. Using the actual cost gap between extended and non-extended stays in this test set ($52,630), a 15% intervention-effectiveness assumption translates to roughly $102,629 in illustrative savings across the 13 correctly-flagged cases — a directional estimate, not a guarantee. SHAP analysis also surfaced non-trivial influence from race on the extended-stay classifier's predictions — an unprompted finding treated as a concrete audit trigger rather than a footnote: before any real deployment, error rates should be compared across race, ethnicity, and payer-type subgroups for disparate false-negative or false-positive rates.
