Length-of-Stay (LOS) Prediction / Cost-of-Care Modeling

Headline Finding

An admission-time-only model flags extended-stay risk with 81% recall — catching 13 of 16 true long-stay cases in the test set — while excluding any information only knowable after a patient is already admitted.

The Problem

A small share of admissions drive a disproportionate share of both length of stay and cost — the top cost decile in this data accounts for 78.4% of total spend. The operational question isn't whether that's true, it's whether it can be flagged early enough to act on: at admission, not partway through the stay.

The Approach

Features were deliberately scoped to information available at the moment of admission — age, comorbidity count, prior admissions, encounter type — excluding anything that only becomes known mid-stay (procedures performed, new diagnoses), even though including those features barely moved the accuracy numbers on this sample. A model that "predicts" length of stay using information from partway through the stay isn't usable at the decision point that actually matters. Three models were built on top of this feature set: LOS regression, extended-stay classification, and cost regression, each evaluated against a simple baseline.

The Outcome

The extended-stay classifier reached 0.942 ROC-AUC and, at a recall-prioritized threshold, flags 9.2% of admissions for review to catch 81% of true extended stays. The LOS regression model cut mean absolute error by 14.4% over a linear baseline (0.330 vs. 0.385 days). The cost model was reported honestly as the weakest of the three (MAE at 77% of mean cost) — since cost is driven heavily by what happens during a stay, which an admission-time-only model can't see by design. A SHAP fairness check also surfaced non-trivial influence from race on the extended-stay predictions, flagged as a concrete subgroup-audit trigger rather than a generic disclaimer.
