Healthcare Resource Optimization: Beds, Staffing & Equipment

Headline Finding

Heading into winter, a 280-bed hospital was forecast to run at 94% average occupancy, at or above 95% on 93 of 182 days. Reshaping the nurse roster and bed mix cut expected six-month cost by $1.42M, yet shortening average stay by half a day was worth more than any bed addition tested.

The Problem

Capacity decisions were being made one resource at a time, on last year's averages: a flat nurse roster, a fixed bed map, and equipment fleets nobody had re-counted. Averages hide the days that matter. A hospital that is full 94% of the time still turns patients away on its busiest days, pays agency premiums to fill the gap, and over-staffs the quiet ones. The question wasn't how much capacity existed, it was where it was wasted and where it would run out first.

The Approach

Demand was forecast once and pushed through all three resources, since bed demand drives nursing and equipment demand. A Poisson model (trend, annual cycle, weekday pattern) beat naive baselines in a five-origin backtest, at 10% weekly error against 16%. Patients were then simulated through ICU, Step-Down and Med-Surg units across 500 plausible winters, so decisions were judged against the tails, not the average. Optimization sat on top: a nurse roster built as an integer program with agency cover as the fallback, a bed split across units priced by what each unit's lost admission costs, and equipment fleets sized from the stock-out curve. The data is synthetic and the costs are labelled assumptions.

The Outcome

A flat roster left core staff 11 to 18 shifts short every day of a winter week. Re-shaping it to the weekday demand pattern, with 197 core nurses instead of 175, cut agency shifts from about 111 to 22 a week and saved roughly $0.65M. Moving nine beds from Med-Surg toward ICU and Step-Down saved a further $0.9M in expected diversion cost, though total diversions rose slightly, and ICU hit its conversion limit. The 340-pump infusion pool would have been short on about one day in three; sharing one pool of 385 needs 35 fewer pumps than separate unit fleets. The 36 ventilators ran at 34% utilisation, with a cost-optimal fleet nearer 21, but a reserve was recommended for surges. In scenarios, a half-day cut in length of stay saved about $2.9M against the optimised plan and absorbed a 10% demand surge, while twelve flex beds only broke even. Forecast error here is measured on simulated data, so real-world error would be higher.
