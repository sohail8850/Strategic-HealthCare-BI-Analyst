Patient No-Show Prediction Model: From Guessing to Strategic Double-Booking

Headline Finding

On months the model had never seen, padding two random slots per provider-day lost about $2,476 per 100 provider-days, while double-booking only above a 27% break-even risk gained about $3,953. A simple "who missed before" rule captured most of that gain ($3,127); the model added about 26% on top.

The Problem

Clinic schedules are built as if every patient will arrive, and the misses are then absorbed by instinct. Front-desk staff double-book the patients they remember missing before, or a session is padded by a fixed amount for everyone. Both fail in predictable ways. Memory is inconsistent and cannot be audited, and blanket padding crowds the waiting room on the days when most patients turn up. Every empty slot is clinician time that cannot be recovered. The question was never whether to double-book, but which appointments to double-book and how many.

The Approach

A synthetic six-provider clinic was built so the method could be tested end to end: 55,070 appointments over two years, with 18.2% missed. Each appointment was scored for no-show risk using only what is known at booking: the patient's earlier attendance, how far ahead it was booked, age, appointment type, insurance, distance, weekday and time of day. The model was trained on the first 15 months, tuned on the next 3, and tested on the final 6. It ranked unseen appointments with an AUC of 0.71, against 0.68 for ranking by miss history alone and a ceiling of 0.78 for a model that knew every patient's true risk. It was also well calibrated: in the riskiest tenth of appointments it predicted 44.1%, and 43.8% were missed.

The policy rule comes from simple arithmetic. An extra booking recovers an empty slot with probability p (assumed worth $120) and causes a collision with probability 1 − p (assumed cost $45), so it pays only when p exceeds 45 / (120 + 45) = 27%. Three policies were then replayed on the 786 held-out provider-days using the real outcomes, each limited to two extra bookings per provider per day.

Per 100 provider-days on the test months. Hit rate is the share of extra bookings that landed in a real gap. Net value is slots recovered times $120, less collisions times $45, on synthetic data and assumed costs.

Padding at random recovered only 18% of the time, which is just the clinic's base rate, so it lost money. Targeting changed the exchange rate: the model policy filled a real gap 43% of the time. The surprise is how much of the value sat in a patient's own history. The front-desk rule netted $3,127, and the model added $826 per 100 provider-days, roughly $12,900 a year for six providers. Most of the gain came from replacing blanket padding with any targeted rule.

Three results held up under testing. The break-even threshold set from the costs landed within three points of the empirical optimum on both the validation and test months. The model policy stayed positive in 7 of 7 cost scenarios, against 6 for the front-desk rule and 1 for flat padding. And a cap of two extra bookings per provider-day is a limit on waiting, not a profit lever: each extra booking above the threshold still paid, but with shrinking returns and rising collisions.

One result needs care. Flags were not spread evenly: Medicaid patients were 21% of appointments but 39% of flags, and patients aged 18 to 30 were 22% of appointments but 42% of flags. The flags were about equally precise across groups, so the model is not wrongly accusing one group, but a flag should add a booking, never remove one, and the short-notice patient should be the one who waits.

The result is consistent with published work. A model built on 1.26 million real appointments found the same structure: flagged appointments were about three times as likely to be missed as average (18% against 6%), yet still more likely to be kept than missed, so its authors capped overbooking at two per provider per day.

Scope and evidence. All results come from a synthetic clinic with assumed costs. They show how the method works, not what a real clinic should expect, and real no-show patterns, waitlists and costs should be tested before use.

Source
